*A large company's logo on a poster tells young participants that someone checked. Here is what that check can look like, using an 8 November 2026 Telkom AI Center event as the example.*

This is a general piece. It draws on one case, a Telkom AI Center of Excellence event I attended on 8 November 2026, but it makes no claim that the organisers skipped any step. I do not know what checks were done. I only know that I never saw them.

For the case itself, see [what happened in the group](/arsip/artikel/telkom-ai-center-indigo-founders-trinity-removed-one-minute-after-fact-check-link).

## Why a logo carries weight

The poster for that event shows Danantara Indonesia, AI Connect, Telkom AI Center of Excellence and Telkom Indonesia at the top, with community partners at the bottom. A student scrolling past it will reasonably assume the speaker was vetted.

That assumption is the product. So the organiser owes participants the vetting that the logo implies.

## A checklist that fits in an afternoon

For every external speaker whose name appears next to a corporate brand:

1. **List each claim that will be used to promote the speaker.** Degrees, affiliations, funding, user numbers, clients. If it is on the poster, the bio or the speaker's own profile that the audience will read, it belongs on the list.
2. **Check each claim against a primary source.** For a degree or enrolment, use the national higher-education database and ask the speaker for a letter from the institution. For a company, use the company registry. For "research student" or "visiting" titles, ask for a letter from the supervisor or lab, because the title alone does not define a relationship.
3. **Write down what could not be verified.** "Not found" is not "false", and the right action is usually to drop the unverified line from the promotion, not to cancel the speaker.
4. **Check the collaborators too.** A company that appears as "In Collaboration With" is part of the endorsement. Record who it is and whether anyone on the organising side has a relationship with it.
5. **Decide in advance who answers a participant's warning.** One named person, one channel, one reply time.
6. **Keep the file.** If anyone asks later, the answer should be a document, not a recollection.

## Handling a participant who raises a flag

Whatever the flag turns out to be worth, a participant who brings a source in good faith is giving the organiser free quality control. Three responses cost almost nothing:

- Acknowledge in the same channel: "Thanks, we are checking."
- Ask the participant to send the source privately, so the live chat is not used for accusations.
- Reply with the result once it is known, even if the result is "we verified, it is fine."

Removal is also a legitimate tool. A moderator can remove someone who posts accusations into a live group, and that is not necessarily a breach of anything. But removal alone answers the wrong question. It settles who is in the group, not whether the claim is true.

## Where I may be wrong

- I do not know what checks Telkom AI Center of Excellence or AI Connect ran. They may have run all of the above.
- Community events are often run by small teams on short notice, and a partner logo may reflect sponsorship rather than endorsement of each speaker.
- My own message in the group was blunt. A calmer message, sent privately, might have led to a different outcome.

## What I asked for

I asked the Telkom Integrity Line to review the speaker selection and to verify credentials from primary sources. I did not ask for a penalty.
