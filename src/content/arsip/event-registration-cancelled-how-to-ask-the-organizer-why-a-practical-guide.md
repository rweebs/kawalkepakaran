*A cancellation email with no reason is common, and it is easy to guess wrong about who did it. Here is a short, safe process that gets an answer without accusing anyone, using the DevFest Jakarta 2026 case as the example.*

This guide draws on my own experience with [a cancelled DevFest Jakarta 2026 registration](/arsip/artikel/gdg-jakarta-devfest-jakarta-2026-registration-cancelled-by-organizer-what-the-record-shows). It is general advice, not legal advice.

## Step 1: Read what the email actually says

Write down, word for word: the sender, the date and time, the subject and the sentence that says what happened. In my case: "cancelled by the organizer and you have been removed from the event." That tells you the **organization** acted. It does not tell you which **person** did, or whether a system did.

## Step 2: Check the boring explanations first

- **Payment status.** Look at any invoice for amount paid and due date. Unpaid orders are often released automatically.
- **Capacity.** Free or early-bird places may be capped.
- **Duplicate or invalid registration.** The same email or phone used twice.
- **Eligibility rules.** Some events screen applicants.

None of these needs a person with a grudge. Rule them out before you form a theory about anyone.

## Step 3: Ask the organizer in writing

Reply to the cancellation email, or write to the address it came from. Keep it short and neutral. A template:

> Hello, I received a notice on [date, time] that my registration for [event] was cancelled by the organizer. Could you tell me (1) the reason, (2) whether it was automatic or manual, and (3) whether I can be reinstated? My order number and proof of payment status are attached. Thank you.

Attach: the cancellation email, the invoice, and any payment proof. Do not name or guess at an individual.

## Step 4: Set a time limit and keep a record

Give a reasonable window, for example three working days. Keep screenshots of the emails with their headers and times. Redact your phone number, email address and invoice numbers before sharing any image.

## Step 5: Escalate with facts, not with names

If there is no answer, use the organizer's official public channels: the community's social accounts, its contact form, or its parent organization. State the facts and the question. Avoid naming a speaker, volunteer or staff member unless there is a document that ties them to the decision.

## What not to do

- **Do not assume a person caused it.** A speaker on the poster is not necessarily on the organising team. In my case, Alvin Prayuda Juniarta Dwiyantoro is named on the poster as a workshop speaker, and no document I hold ties him to the cancellation. I wondered about him once, and the [record](/arsip/artikel/alvin-prayuda-juniarta-dwiyantoro-devfest-jakarta-2026-what-the-record-does-and-does-not-show) shows why that was a guess and not a finding.
- **Do not read a blank page as a block.** On LinkedIn, "This page doesn't exist" can mean a deleted profile, a changed URL, or a block. The page alone does not say which.
- **Do not publish accusations before you have asked.** A public accusation closes the door on a simple correction.
- **Do not post an unredacted screenshot.** Phone numbers and invoice numbers can be misused.

## Where this advice may fall short

Some organizers do not reply, and in that case a public, factual record may be the only option left. Even then, the record should say what the documents show and what they do not.
